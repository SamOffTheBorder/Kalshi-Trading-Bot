Executive Summary
This report analyzes a massive computational experiment utilizing Anthropic's Claude to backtest over 9,000
trading strategies across 15 years of daily market data. By running these strategies through a rigorous
"Validation Gauntlet," the experiment sought to separate genuine mathematical edges from curve-fitted
anomalies. The decisive conclusion: Mean Reversion is the only broad strategy family with a persistent,
statistically significant edge in naked form, whereas Trend Following and Momentum strategies exhibit
dangerous fragility and path-dependency unless enhanced by dynamic regime-switching models. 
1. Experimental Architecture & Data Parameters
To avoid finding isolated anomalies that only work in today's unique economic climate, the experimental
framework was designed to be intentionally harsh, utilizing broad time horizons and highly liquid assets.
Asset Universe: 30 highly liquid instruments. This includes major market indices (S&P 500, NASDAQ),
sector-specific ETFs, commodities (Gold, Oil), fixed income (Bonds), cryptocurrencies (Bitcoin, Ethereum),
and mega-cap individual equities (Apple, Nvidia).
Time Horizon: 15 years of daily price data (bars). This period encapsulates multiple distinct market
cycles, including bull runs, choppy consolidations, and major macroeconomic crashes.
Strategy Format: "Naked" or base strategies. The algorithms were tested in their purest forms—without
secondary confirmation indicators, arbitrary stop-loss optimization, or dynamic sizing—to determine if
the underlying hypothesis actually held merit.
Excluded Domains: Intraday trading, futures leverage, and options structures were excluded to keep the
focus purely on directional, large-scale asset movement.
2. The Validation Gauntlet: Filtering the Noise
The core innovation of this experiment was the implementation of a  Walk-Forward Testing methodology.
Standard backtesting optimizes parameters over a historical period, leading to curve-fitting. Walk-forward
testing builds and tunes the strategy on an older subset of data (In-Sample), and then immediately evaluates it
on a newer, unseen subset of data (Out-Of-Sample). If the edge is real, the performance should carry over.

The Overfitting Crisis
A scatterplot analysis comparing In-Sample (IS) vs Out-Of-Sample (OOS) performance revealed a stark truth:
Of all the strategies that looked highly profitable on historical training data, only 44% maintained their
profitability on unseen data. This demonstrates that over half of "successful" backtests—such as those
commonly generated on basic retail platforms like TradingView—are functionally fake. They have merely
memorized random historical noise. 
3. Strategy Archetypes: Trend vs. Mean Reversion
The  surviving  strategies  were  categorized  into  distinct  "families"  representing  fundamentally  opposite
philosophies of market behavior.
Trend Following & Momentum
These strategies hypothesize that an object in motion stays in motion. If an asset is breaking out or exhibiting
strong relative strength, the algorithm buys with the expectation that the trend will continue. Examples
include Moving Average Crossovers and Breakout Systems.

Mean Reversion
These strategies hypothesize that asset prices behave like a rubber band. When prices stretch too far from their
historical average (due to panic selling or euphoric buying), they will inevitably snap back. Examples include
RSI (Relative Strength Index) Snapbacks, Bollinger Band reversions, and Keltner Channel pullbacks.
4. Results: The Dominance of Mean Reversion
When  the  results  were  aggregated,  Mean  Reversion  was  the  only  strategy  category  that  maintained  a
positive average return across the entire 15-year dataset . Trend, Volume, Composite, Volatility, and Pattern-
based strategies were all negative on average in their base forms.
Broad Survivability: Out of the 524 surviving strategies, filtering for assets with over 10 years of history
left 478 robust algorithms. Of these, a staggering 64% were Mean Reversion strategies.
Cross-Asset Viability: The RSI Mean Reversion strategy did not just work on one lucky asset; it
independently survived the gauntlet across 20 completely unrelated tickers. Similarly, Keltner Reversion
survived across 18 distinct tickers.
Theoretical Backing: This empirical data aligns perfectly with decades of academic quantitative research.
Mean reversion exploits deeply ingrained human psychological flaws (fear and greed causing
overreactions) and market microstructure mechanics, giving it a durable edge that random moving
average crossovers lack.
5. The Fragility of Trend Following
It is crucial to note that Trend Following is not entirely broken. In fact, the single highest-scoring strategy in
the entire experiment was a "Turtle Trend" breakout system applied specifically to Apple (AAPL), achieving
an impressive Sharpe ratio of 1.18.
However,  the  experiment  highlighted  extreme  fragility  in  momentum  models  via  Bootstrapping  (Monte
Carlo Analysis) . By taking the winning trades of a strategy and randomly reshuffling their sequence 500
times, the researchers tested whether the strategy was structurally sound or merely lucky.
Mean Reversion under Bootstrapping: Maintained a solid, positive verdict across alternate realities.
Momentum under Bootstrapping: A highly successful Dual Momentum strategy on Nvidia (NVDA)
showed devastating fragility. When trade order was reshuffled, the strategy exhibited maximum
drawdowns of -61% and -51% in alternate paths. The strategy only looked safe because the market
followed one specific, lucky historical sequence.
The Fix for Momentum:  The basic time-series momentum (buying a single asset because it is going up)
failed. However, testing Cross-Sectional Momentum—ranking an entire basket of assets, going long on the top
performers, and shorting the weakest—yielded significantly superior and more robust results.

6. Architecting a Professional Trading System
The foundational lesson of the 9,000-strategy test is that naked algorithms are merely raw materials. To build
an institutional-grade automated trading system, one must construct it in sequential layers:
Layer 1: The Base Edge. Start with a fundamentally sound, statistically validated signal like RSI Mean
Reversion.
Layer 2: Risk Management & Sizing. Apply position sizing algorithms (e.g., Kelly Criterion) and strict
drawdown limits tailored to the trader's specific capital base and psychological risk tolerance.
Layer 3: Uncorrelated Signals. Combine multiple base edges that do not move in tandem. One weak edge
is noise; several uncorrelated weak edges combined create a robust, smoothed equity curve.
Layer 4: Regime Detection (Hidden Markov Models). This is the apex of quantitative design. Because
strategies are highly situational, use AI-driven Hidden Markov Models (HMMs) to detect the current
"state" of the market. 
Trending / Bull Regime: Activate Cross-Sectional Momentum strategies.
Choppy / Ranging Regime: Activate Mean Reversion strategies.
7. Replication Framework via LLMs
The entire Python-based testing apparatus was written using Anthropic's Claude, demonstrating how retail
traders can leverage AI to perform hedge-fund-level quantitative research. The architecture requires four
distinct prompt layers:
Prompt 1: Data & Strategy Library: Establishes API connections to fetch the 30 assets and defines the
mathematical rules for the base strategies.
Prompt 2: Backtest Engine: Implements the Walk-Forward optimization loop, strictly segregating In-
Sample and Out-Of-Sample data.
Prompt 3: Filters & Validation: Applies realistic transaction costs, slippage penalties, and executes the 6-
step funnel (Sharpe, Drawdown, etc.).
Prompt 4: Robustness & Regimes: Integrates the Monte Carlo bootstrapping sequence and the Hidden
Markov Model logic for regime-switching.
